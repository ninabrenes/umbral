# indigenous psychedelic traditions and ethical reference guide

research compiled for the-portal: an open-source psychedelic preparation and integration tool.
this document is a living reference for how the portal should (and should not) engage with indigenous knowledge systems.

---

## part 1: major traditions

### 1. mazatec psilocybin ceremonies (veladas)

**the people:** the mazatec are an indigenous people of the sierra mazateca in oaxaca, mexico. they have used psilocybin mushrooms (ndi xijtho, "little ones that sprout") for centuries as conscious entities capable of communicating wisdom and healing, not merely as substances.

**the velada ceremony:**
- nocturnal healing vigils led by curanderos/curanderas (healers) or sabios/sabias (wise ones)
- not recreational. a multi-day introspective and therapeutic process
- mushrooms are always presented in pairs (believed to be married). only one species consumed at a time
- traditionally only conducted during mushroom season, always with fresh-harvested specimens
- elements: prayer, chanting in "the language of the saints," copal incense, candles, aguardiente, tobacco
- the healer ingests twice the dose of participants and guides the experience through poetic vocalization
- purpose: commune with the divine, heal the sick, receive guidance

**preparation protocols:**
- ritual cleansings (limpias) including fasting from certain foods, drinks, and sexual activity
- the space, the participants, and the mushrooms themselves are all ritually cleansed
- herbs, copal, candles, and incense used in preparation

**maria sabina (1894-1985):**
- mazatec sabia (wise woman) and poet from huautla de jimenez, oaxaca
- used primarily psilocybe caerulescens in her veladas
- R. Gordon Wasson published her work in Life magazine (1957) without understanding the consequences
- the publicity was catastrophic: her house was burned, her brother murdered, she was jailed and ostracized
- she died in poverty from malnutrition in 1985
- her story is the foundational cautionary tale about extraction of indigenous knowledge

**what the portal must understand:** the velada is a complete cosmological system, not a "mushroom trip." the mushrooms are intermediaries between humans and the divine. removing them from this context is not "simplification," it is erasure.

### 2. shipibo-conibo ayahuasca traditions

**the people:** the shipibo-conibo (also shipibo-konibo) are an indigenous people of the ucayali river region of the peruvian amazon. they are among the most recognized ayahuasca traditions globally.

**icaros (healing songs):**
- not composed or invented. learned directly from plants during training and fasting
- the song is the vibration of the plant translated into melody
- sung primarily in the shipibo-conibo language
- healing happens through vibration and intention, not through intellectual comprehension of lyrics
- each icaro carries specific healing properties tied to specific plants and conditions
- icaros are considered the primary tool of healing, more important than the ayahuasca itself

**the dieta (plant diet):**
- extended periods of isolation, fasting, and communion with specific "teacher plants" (plantas maestras)
- bland diet free from salt, sugar, oils, spices. no alcohol, meat, or sexual activity
- the dieta is how healers build their relationship with plant spirits over years and decades
- different master plants teach different things. the healer accumulates relationships over a lifetime
- the shipibo specialize in the dieta as a spiritual technology

**the curandero/curandera (onanya in shipibo):**
- train for decades through successive plant dietas
- act as conduits for plant spirit wisdom, not as the source of healing themselves
- the ceremony is held at night in darkness
- the onanya drinks ayahuasca and sings icaros to guide, protect, and heal participants
- the relationship between healer and plants is built through years of sacrifice and discipline

**what the portal must understand:** the shipibo system is relational, not transactional. the healer has a decades-long relationship with plant spirits. an app cannot replicate or substitute for this relationship. any reference to "icaros" or "dieta" must acknowledge they belong to a living tradition with living practitioners.

### 3. wixarika (huichol) peyote traditions

**the people:** the wixarika (self-name; "huichol" is the Spanish-imposed name) live in the sierra madre occidental across nayarit, jalisco, zacatecas, and durango in mexico.

**hikuri (peyote):**
- the peyote cactus (lophophora williamsii) is not a "drug" to the wixarika. it is a sacred being
- without hikuri, wixarika culture essentially ceases to exist. their beliefs, art, and history are defined by this relationship
- hikuri is a tool to dialogue with the ancient spirits

**the pilgrimage to wirikuta:**
- annual 800km pilgrimage to wirikuta in the san luis potosi desert (real de catorce region)
- traditionally a 17-day walk with fasting, eating only fruits found along the way
- ritual renaming: the desert becomes the ocean, cars become ships, donkeys become giraffes. this mental preparation rewires perception before the peyote ceremony
- the pilgrimage unifies families and communities, reinforces cultural values, and serves individual spiritual development
- modern threats: mining interests, drug traffickers, and private property have made the walking route nearly impossible. most wixaritari now travel by bus or truck

**cultural significance:**
- pilgrimage connects three sacred points: the pacific coast (where the sun sets), the sierra (where they live), and wirikuta (where the sun was born)
- this geography IS their cosmology. the land is not a backdrop; it is the teaching itself

**what the portal must understand:** the wixarika relationship with hikuri is inseparable from their land, their pilgrimage, and their cosmology. peyote cannot be meaningfully discussed outside this total context. the wixarika are actively fighting to protect wirikuta from mining and development. any reference to their tradition should acknowledge this ongoing struggle.

### 4. santo daime and uniao do vegetal (UDV)

**santo daime:**
- founded in the 1930s in acre, brazil by raimundo irineu serra (mestre irineu)
- syncretic religion blending: folk catholicism, kardecist spiritism, african animism (orixas like ogum and iemanja), and indigenous amazonian shamanism (vegetalismo)
- ayahuasca is called "daime" (from "dai-me" = "give me" in portuguese)
- drinking daime has the same significance as the catholic eucharist. they believe they are drinking in jesus christ
- ceremonies involve hymns (hinarios), dancing in formation, and collective spiritual work
- christian core with animist appreciation of nature, indigenous spirit beings, and afro-brazilian deities

**uniao do vegetal (UDV):**
- founded 1961 by jose gabriel da costa (mestre gabriel) in porto velho, brazil
- calls ayahuasca "vegetal" (hoasca)
- more structured organizational hierarchy than santo daime
- focus on Christ's teachings alongside amazonian heritage
- won a landmark US supreme court case in 2006 (gonzales v. o centro espirita) allowing ritual use

**what makes these traditions distinct:**
- they are not indigenous traditions. they are syncretic religions that emerged from the encounter between indigenous amazonian knowledge, african diaspora spirituality, and european christianity
- they represent a genuine cultural synthesis, not appropriation, because they arose organically within brazilian communities that are themselves products of cultural mixing
- they have formal structures, doctrines, and legal protections

**what the portal must understand:** santo daime and UDV demonstrate that syncretic traditions CAN emerge respectfully, but they did so through generations of lived community practice, not through weekend workshops. the key difference: these traditions were created by communities that included indigenous and african-descended peoples within their own cultural context.

### 5. bwiti ibogaine traditions (gabon)

**the people:** the bwiti tradition originated among the pygmy peoples (particularly the mbenge ethnic group in gabon, including aka, gyele, bongo, baka, and kola groups). it has been adopted by the fang and other ethnic groups.

**iboga:**
- tabernanthe iboga. the root bark is consumed, particularly the second layer
- according to tradition, pygmies first observed the effects when mountain gorillas chewed the root
- iboga is not a casual substance. it is profoundly intense and can be medically dangerous

**the initiation ceremony:**
- the crucial rite of bwiti. young gabonese men and women take iboga for the first time
- conducted in gender-specific huts
- lasts 3-7 days depending on the region
- characteristics: monotonous repetitive music, purification, vomiting, fasting, censing (smoke/incense), receiving the iboga root
- music and dance are central. instruments include drums, shakers, the ngombi harp, and the mongongo

**the n'ganga (spiritual leader):**
- very important community member
- extensive knowledge of traditional healing practices
- guides initiates through the visionary experience
- responsible for the safety and spiritual development of the community

**what the portal must understand:** bwiti initiation is a once-in-a-lifetime community rite of passage. the western pattern of repeated ibogaine use for addiction treatment, while potentially valuable medically, bears almost no relationship to the bwiti ceremonial context. any reference to bwiti must acknowledge this fundamental difference.

## part 2: what indigenous communities are saying

### 6. the chacruna institute and indigenous reciprocity initiative (IRI)

**chacruna's mission:** promotes psychedelic justice by uplifting voices of women, queer people, indigenous peoples, people of color, and the global south in psychedelic science. curates critical conversations about reciprocity.

**the indigenous reciprocity initiative (IRI):**
- grassroots network of 20 indigenous organizations across the US, mexico, costa rica, colombia, ecuador, peru, and brazil
- channels resources directly to indigenous communities, bypassing large bureaucracies
- contributions equally distributed to all 20 organizations (7.5% admin fee to chacruna)
- 77.6% of contributions in 2024 were under $50, showing grassroots support
- supports everything from food security to land tenure to cultural conservation
- critically: not every group in IRI explicitly uses psychedelic plants. this decouples indigenous support from the "psychedelic exotic" framing

**key insight from chacruna:** "reciprocity is really a constant process of balancing that acknowledges one's place in an ever-shifting story of relationship." it is not a one-time donation. it is an ongoing orientation.

**the portal should:** link to and encourage contributions to the IRI. consider building in a reciprocity prompt or mechanism.

### 7. indigenous community statements

**mazatec community:**
- deep ambivalence about outsiders. those offering ceremonies are either valued or despised depending on context
- mazatecs report being exploited by "facilitators" who come to learn from neoshamans, then offer ceremonies for profit elsewhere
- maria sabina's story remains the defining example of what goes wrong

**shipibo-conibo community:**
- the declaration of yarinacocha (2018): shipibo healers organized to resist "spiritual extractivism"
- ASOMASHK (association of shipibo healers) demands that all national and international events related to ancestral medicine require their consent
- coshikox (collective of shipibo-konibo-xetebo peoples) stated: majority of foreign-owned ayahuasca centers exploit employees, pay unfair salaries, neglect health issues, and show no concern for transmission of knowledge to younger generations
- shipibo leaders demand: legal protection for sacred plant medicines, recognition of indigenous intellectual property rights, ethical tourism standards
- central question from the declaration: "will the enlightened and the healed help the overwhelming fight against big companies eating up the rainforest and destroying the territorial survival of the very shipibo communities from whom they are receiving so much benefit?"

**the portal should:** present these statements directly. not paraphrase or soften them. the discomfort is the point.

### 8-9. reciprocity vs. appropriation: where is the line?

**appropriation looks like:**
- removing practices from their cultural context and using them for personal gain
- claiming initiation into traditions after brief exposure
- treating medicines as divorced from their communities
- becoming "neo-shamans" without deep, authentic study within proper lineage
- frequent ceremony use (e.g., every weekend) vs. indigenous cultures where one ceremony in a lifetime is significant
- using sacred imagery decoratively (kene patterns on yoga mats, icaros as spotify playlists)
- patenting traditional medicines or processes

**reciprocity looks like:**
- a constant process of balancing and acknowledging relationship
- channeling resources directly to indigenous communities
- supporting land protection and territorial sovereignty
- ensuring indigenous communities have authority over how their knowledge is shared
- recognizing indigenous peoples as knowledge holders, not data sources
- free, prior, and informed consent (FPIC) for any use of traditional knowledge
- benefit-sharing that reaches frontline communities

**the line:** appropriation extracts value and moves it away from the source community. reciprocity creates a flow that returns value to the source. the question is always: who benefits? if the answer is only "us," that is appropriation.

## part 3: the eight ethical principles (lancet 2023)

an indigenous-led global consensus published in the lancet regional health - americas. these are the authoritative ethical framework for western engagement with indigenous psychedelic knowledge.

### organized in four categories:

**acknowledgment:**
1. **reverence for mother nature** — commitment to ecosystem protection. shift from anthropocentric (human-centered) to kincentric (relational) approaches. gratitude and stewardship, not extraction.
2. **respect indigenous ways of knowing and being** — uphold FPIC. fund indigenous ethical review bodies. support community-developed biocultural protocols. indigenous leadership, not consultation.

**knowledge-translation and education:**
3. **responsibility for use, benefits, harms** — institutional accountability for preventing cultural appropriation. formal organizational reviews. indigenous representation on ethics boards.
4. **relevance of indigenous knowledges** — elevate indigenous perspectives as essential, not supplementary. employ indigenous scholars in leadership roles. honor context-relevant knowledge translation.

**intellectual property and rights:**
5. **regulation of tangible and intangible uses** — strict legal protections for indigenous intellectual property. constitutional recognition determined by indigenous self-governance.
6. **reparation and sharing of benefits** — equitable benefit distribution. actively restitute cultural and spiritual property with indigenous consent.

**belonging:**
7. **restoration of indigenous authority** — position indigenous knowledge holders as guiding voices, not advisors. recognize indigenous systems as equal partners.
8. **reconciliation of indigenous-western relations** — restorative and transformative justice. participatory methodologies that respect local autonomy.

**the portal should:** use these eight principles as a design checklist. every feature that references indigenous knowledge should be evaluated against them.

## part 4: how to respectfully reference without appropriating

### 10. attribution practices

**do:**
- name the specific tradition and people (e.g., "shipibo-conibo tradition" not "shamanic tradition")
- use the people's own name for themselves (wixarika, not huichol, unless explaining both)
- cite specific communities and organizations where possible
- link to indigenous-led organizations and resources
- include context about the tradition's current situation (threats, legal battles, economic conditions)
- use language like "according to [tradition]" rather than presenting indigenous knowledge as universal fact

**don't:**
- use generic terms like "shamanic," "ancient wisdom," or "indigenous ceremony" without specificity
- present indigenous knowledge as a monolith. these are distinct nations with distinct practices
- use past tense for living traditions ("the mazatec believed..." vs "the mazatec practice...")

### 11. what NOT to do

- co-opt ceremonies: don't create "digital veladas" or "virtual icaros experiences"
- use sacred imagery decoratively: no kene patterns as UI elements, no peyote symbols as icons, no bwiti masks as illustrations
- claim lineage: never suggest the tool is "in the tradition of" or "based on" a specific indigenous practice
- flatten differences: don't merge distinct traditions into a generic "indigenous wisdom" category
- romanticize poverty: don't frame indigenous communities as "simple" or "pure" while ignoring the material conditions they face
- spiritual tourism framing: don't position indigenous traditions as "experiences" to be consumed
- use indigenous language terms casually: words like "icaro," "velada," "dieta" carry weight. use them with context or not at all

### 12. how ethical platforms handle this

**ICEERS (international center for ethnobotanical education, research and service):**
- centers indigenous voices as participants, not subjects
- collaborates with indigenous organizations like UMIYAC (union of indigenous yage doctors of the colombian amazon)
- frames indigenous knowledge as living tradition requiring ongoing relationship
- does not present indigenous rituals as "cultural embellishment"

**chacruna institute:**
- indigenous reciprocity initiative channels direct resources
- amplifies indigenous voices in their own words
- hosts conferences that include indigenous leaders as speakers, not subjects
- publishes indigenous perspectives (e.g., mazatec perspectives on psilocybin globalization)

**heroic hearts project:**
- focuses on veteran access to psychedelic therapy
- works with established retreat centers rather than creating pseudo-indigenous experiences
- does not claim indigenous lineage for their therapeutic approach

**common pattern among ethical organizations:** they acknowledge the debt, name the source traditions, channel resources back, and do not claim to BE those traditions.

### 13. "inspired by" vs. "taken from"

**"inspired by" (acceptable with care):**
- acknowledging that preparation practices exist in indigenous traditions and creating modern parallels
- saying "many indigenous traditions emphasize set and setting. here is how to think about yours"
- referencing the concept of reciprocity and encouraging it
- noting that integration has deep roots in indigenous post-ceremony practices

**"taken from" (not acceptable):**
- lifting specific ceremonial elements (icaros, limpias, specific prayers) and embedding them in the tool
- using indigenous terminology as feature names (e.g., "your digital velada," "icaro player")
- reproducing kene patterns, huichol yarn art, or bwiti iconography as UI decoration
- creating "ceremony modes" that simulate indigenous rituals
- extracting the "useful parts" of a tradition while discarding the cosmology, community, and commitment that give those parts meaning

**the test:** if an indigenous person from that tradition would look at your feature and feel their culture had been reduced to a product feature, it's "taken from." if they would feel their tradition had been acknowledged and respected while you built something genuinely your own, it's "inspired by."

## part 5: the bilingual EN/ES context

### 14. how spanish changes the relationship

**proximity, not distance:** for spanish-speaking users, especially in latin america, these are not "exotic foreign traditions." they are the traditions of their own continent, sometimes their own country, sometimes their own family.

**implications for the portal:**
- spanish-language content can engage more directly with tradition names, concepts, and contexts because there is less cultural distance to bridge
- BUT this proximity also creates risk: spanish-speaking users may assume familiarity equals permission
- mestizo identity is complex. many latin americans have both indigenous and spanish heritage, creating genuine but complicated claims to these traditions
- the colonial history matters: curanderismo survived BECAUSE indigenous practitioners hid their practices inside catholic frameworks during spanish colonization. the spanish language itself was the language of suppression

**language-specific considerations:**
- use terms like "curandero/curandera" naturally in spanish content (they are spanish words already integrated into the culture)
- in english content, explain and contextualize these terms
- be aware that "chamán" (shaman) is actually a siberian word adopted into spanish. prefer tradition-specific terms: onanya (shipibo), sabia/sabio (mazatec), marakame (wixarika), n'ganga (bwiti)
- respect that some indigenous terms are in languages that predate spanish and should not be casually hispanicized

### 15. curanderismo: the bridge tradition

**what it is:** a syncretic healing system practiced across mexico and central america since at least the 1400s. blends aztec/indigenous plant knowledge, spanish folk medicine, catholic ritual, and in some regions african spiritual elements.

**why it matters for the portal:**
- curanderismo IS the lived example of indigenous and modern integration. it happened organically over centuries
- it treats illness as arising from physiological, psychological, spiritual, relational, or environmental disharmonies. this holistic view is exactly what psychedelic preparation and integration address
- practitioners (curanderos/curanderas) use herbs, rituals, limpias (cleansings), and spiritual counsel
- currently experiencing a revival, especially among younger generations reconnecting with their cultural heritage
- some healthcare systems are beginning to integrate curanderos as part of multidisciplinary teams

**for the spanish-language portal:** curanderismo provides a culturally resonant framework that many latin american users will recognize from their own families and communities. this is not appropriation when used in this context. it is cultural continuity.

**caution:** curanderismo is not monolithic. it varies enormously by region, practitioner, and lineage. don't flatten it into a single "tradition."

### 16. costa rica's specific context

**legal status of ayahuasca:** neither legal nor illegal. unscheduled substance. however, organizing or charging for ceremonies can carry 8-15 years imprisonment. the legal ambiguity has made costa rica a hub for retreat centers.

**indigenous traditions in costa rica:**
- the bribri people of the talamanca region have their own plant medicine and ceremonial traditions
- cacao holds sacred significance for the bribri. it is a gift from sibu (creator god). used in ceremonies led by shamans, prepared exclusively by women
- the bribri shaman (awa) uses traditional knowledge of plants and rituals for healing and spiritual communication
- the boruca (brunca) people maintain cultural traditions including the fiesta de los diablitos, symbolizing resistance to spanish colonization

**costa rica's retreat economy:**
- many centers bring in shipibo healers from peru, creating a displacement of tradition from its geographic and cultural context
- soltara, rythmia, and others operate in costa rica with varying degrees of ethical engagement
- the IRI includes costa rican indigenous organizations in its network

**for the portal (built from costa rica):**
- acknowledge the specific territory you are building from
- recognize that costa rica's indigenous traditions (bribri, boruca, cabecar, maleku, etc.) exist and deserve acknowledgment even though they are less commercially prominent than amazonian ayahuasca
- the retreat economy in costa rica is part of the problem the portal should help address by encouraging preparation, discernment, and reciprocity rather than consumption

## part 6: practical design guidelines for the portal

### what to build

1. **an indigenous traditions reference section** that presents each tradition on its own terms, in its own context, with links to indigenous-led organizations
2. **a reciprocity mechanism** — link to IRI, suggest contributions, make it easy to give back
3. **a "before you go" guide** for users considering ceremony that includes: questions to ask facilitators about their training and relationship with source traditions, red flags for exploitative retreat centers, what ethical engagement looks like
4. **attribution in every relevant section** — when discussing set/setting, preparation, or integration, name the traditions where these concepts originate
5. **an "about our approach" page** that transparently explains what the tool does and does not claim to be

### what NOT to build

1. no "ceremony simulator" or "digital ritual" features
2. no indigenous iconography or art as UI decoration
3. no "shamanic" branding or terminology
4. no feature names borrowed from specific traditions (no "velada mode," no "dieta tracker," no "icaro library")
5. no generic "ancient wisdom" framing
6. no claim to be "based on" or "rooted in" any specific tradition

### language guidelines

| instead of | use |
|---|---|
| shamanic | traditional healing / ceremonial |
| ancient wisdom | indigenous knowledge systems |
| plant medicine ceremony | ceremony (or specify: ayahuasca ceremony, psilocybin ceremony) |
| shaman | healer / practitioner / use tradition-specific terms with context |
| the indigenous perspective | perspectives from [specific nation] |
| sacred ritual | ceremony or practice (specify which tradition) |
| spiritual journey | experience / process / ceremony |
| medicine work | psychedelic experience / ceremony / therapeutic session |

### the portal's ethical position

the portal is a modern digital tool for preparation and integration. it is NOT:
- a replacement for indigenous ceremonial contexts
- a gateway to "shamanic experiences"
- a source of indigenous knowledge
- a certification that someone is "prepared" for ceremony

the portal IS:
- a tool that helps people prepare thoughtfully
- a resource that directs people to primary sources (including indigenous organizations)
- a platform that models reciprocity by naming its debts and encouraging users to give back
- a bilingual tool that respects the cultural specificity of both its english and spanish-speaking users
